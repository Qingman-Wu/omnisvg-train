\section{Introduction}

Scalable Vector Graphics (SVG) offer a compact, semantically editable, and resolution-independent representation for visual content, and are widely used in graphic design, web development, UI prototyping, and digital illustration. Unlike raster images, SVGs encode a scene as a sequence of drawing commands---paths, curves, fills, and layering operations---and can therefore be viewed as \emph{visual programs}. Recent progress in text-to-SVG has largely followed the autoregressive route: models serialize SVGs into token sequences and generate them token by token~\cite{OmniSVG, SVGen, InternSVG}. This formulation is elegant and scalable, but it becomes brittle once prompts require many parts, long-range spatial coordination, and deeper structural hierarchy.

We observe three recurring failure modes in current text-to-SVG decoders. First, \emph{part omission}: semantically necessary components such as limbs, accessories, or background elements are simply dropped. Second, \emph{layout drift}: spatial relationships degrade over long decoding horizons, producing misplaced or disproportionate compositions. Third, \emph{repetitive strokes}: the decoder falls into local loops and emits redundant drawing commands. These failures are not isolated artifacts; they are symptoms of a shared bottleneck: the absence of visual grounding during autoregressive decoding. SVG generation sits at an unusual interface between language and vision. Its syntax is sequential and text-like, making it amenable to language modeling, yet the object being generated is fundamentally visual, requiring the model to maintain composition, geometry, and part structure over a long sequence. 
Text prompts specify \emph{what} to draw, but provide little structural evidence for \emph{how} to compose the SVG program. This suggests that effective text-to-SVG generation requires structured visual evidence during decoding, rather than text conditioning alone. We therefore recast text-to-SVG as a problem of grounded SVG program synthesis, in which the decoder is guided by visual evidence rather than text alone.



Existing approaches leave this gap only partially addressed. As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:paradigm}, prior methods can be organized along two axes: whether the generator has access to a \emph{direct visual reference} during generation, and whether the method leverages \emph{external knowledge}. \emph{(a) Text-conditioned} methods~\cite{OmniSVG, SVGen, InternSVG} preserve efficient single-pass decoding, but operate without any visual reference. \emph{(b) Score-based optimization} methods~\cite{VectorFusion, SVGDreamer} leverage external priors from pretrained text-to-image models, but the visual signal is only implicit and must be accessed through costly iterative render-and-optimize loops rather than direct reference conditioning. \emph{(c) Self-generated visual conditioning} methods~\cite{DuetSVG} introduce intermediate image tokens as internal visual references, but do so through staged inference and typically retrain the backbone to model a joint image-SVG space. Our method occupies the previously missing quadrant: single-pass autoregressive SVG decoding, after one-time retrieval and encoding, grounded by explicit, structured, externally retrieved visual references.

We introduce \emph{Fetch, Frame, and Focus} (\textbf{F$^{3}$SVG}), a retrieval-augmented framework for decoder-side visual grounding in text-to-SVG generation. Given a text prompt, the \textbf{Fetch} stage retrieves a small set of similar reference SVGs through CLIP-based text similarity search over a FAISS-indexed training corpus, decomposes each into part groups by partitioning paths along drawing order, and converts both the full references and their groups into two sets of visual tokens: \emph{Scene Tokens} that encode scene-level compositional context and \emph{Detail Tokens} that preserve group-level structural evidence. The \textbf{Frame} stage compresses the Scene Tokens into a compact \emph{Panoramic Memory} that captures overall layout, part inventory, and composition. The \textbf{Focus} stage encodes group-level Detail Tokens into addressable \emph{Spotlight Memory} slots that retain fine-grained part structure. During decoding, a lightweight router selects the most relevant spotlight slot for each generation step, while both memory streams are injected into late decoder layers, combining a persistent compositional scaffold with selectively accessed detail evidence.

Beyond introducing a stronger model, we seek to understand how retrieved visual evidence should be integrated into autoregressive SVG decoding. Through systematic ablations over 17 configurations, we distill three design principles: reference evidence should remain structurally decomposed rather than flattened; part-level evidence should be selectively accessed rather than uniformly broadcast; and visual grounding is most effective when injected into late decoder layers rather than spread uniformly across depth. Guided by these principles, our framework adds only 257M trainable parameters (3.3\% of a frozen 7.6B backbone), requires no multimodal retraining of the base model, improves upon the OmniSVG baseline, and achieves performance comparable to frontier general-purpose models such as GPT-5.4 and Claude-4-Sonnet on text-to-SVG generation. Taken together, these results position our method not merely as a stronger OmniSVG variant, but as a lightweight, architecture-preserving framework for retrieval-grounded autoregressive SVG generation.

We summarize our contributions as follows:


\begin{itemize}
  \item We identify the lack of decoder-time visual grounding as a core bottleneck in autoregressive text-to-SVG generation, and present a taxonomy of prior approaches that reveals a missing paradigm: single-pass SVG decoding with explicit, structured, externally retrieved visual evidence.

  \item We propose \emph{Fetch, Frame, and Focus} (F$^{3}$SVG), which retrieves and decomposes reference SVGs, encodes them into complementary Panoramic and Spotlight memories, and injects them into a frozen decoder through adaptive late-layer dual-path grounding.

  \item Through systematic ablations, we derive three generalizable design principles for retrieval-grounded SVG generation: preserve structural decomposition, access part-level evidence selectively, and integrate visual grounding in late decoder layers.

  \item We show that visual grounding can be added in a lightweight, architecture-preserving manner, yielding clear gains over OmniSVG while remaining competitive with frontier general-purpose models on text-to-SVG generation.
\end{itemize}
